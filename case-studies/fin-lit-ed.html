01 / The case for fin-lit

The need is big and growing

Most U.S. adults are under real financial strain, and the data shows it getting worse, not better. The financial education they do get is generic, the same lessons for everyone, so it rarely fits the person in front of it. Targeted financial literacy, tuned to a person's income range, state, community, language, family, and immigration status, barely exists today. This is the case for building it, education grounded in what people report about their own lives, and shaped to actually reach them.

The bigger picture

These figures come from outside our own analysis. In a 2024 survey, U.S. adults said that not understanding money cost them about $1,015 each that year, roughly $243 billion across the country (National Financial Educators Council). At the same time, money apps are one of the fastest growing corners of tech, one forecast puts the market near $208 billion by 2026, growing about 25% a year (Business Research Insights). A large and worsening need, meeting a fast growing market.

02 / The problem

One size fits all keeps failing

Most money apps teach everyone the same way, no matter their income, habits, or where they live. But a lesson that ignores your situation is easy to ignore back. Three points of failure emerge:

Generic content

The same lessons for everyone, ignoring income, habits, and goals.

User fatigue

Too much complexity. People tune out and decide worse.

Context gap

Lessons ignore where someone lives and what it costs.

04 / The strain

Satisfaction down, vulnerability up, worst at the bottom

People felt best about their finances in 2021, thanks to pandemic relief, then that feeling dropped off by 2024. Financial vulnerability moved the opposite way: down in 2021, back up by 2024. Both scores start at the same point (100) in 2009, so they're easy to compare on one chart. Solid lines (satisfaction) trend down, dashed lines (vulnerability) trend up. The lower income band swings the most.

05 / The appetite

Non-White households want it more

Everyone falls into one of three groups: offered financial education and took it, offered it and skipped it, or never offered it at all. Non-White households are offered financial education more often, and say yes to it more often too; they're more engaged and less often left out. The one place White households come out ahead is among people who were already offered it, more of them actually took it, about 74% versus 66%. But far fewer White households are offered it in the first place, so overall, the appetite for it is still stronger in minority communities. That's the audience a targeted platform should serve first. This is shown for 2024, but the same pattern holds all the way back to 2012.

07 / Where this goes next

Beyond broad groups, into a real product

The race categories here are broad. A single "Hispanic" or "Asian" label can hide very different communities inside it, each with different income and homeownership patterns.

  • Hispanic Mexican-origin, Puerto Rican, Cuban, Central American, South American, Dominican
  • Asian Chinese, Indian, Filipino, Vietnamese, Korean, other Asian origins
  • Black U.S.-born multi-generational, Caribbean immigrant, African immigrant
  • White Multi-generational U.S.-born, recent European immigrant, MENA origin
The data gap

How recently someone immigrated shapes money habits more than a broad race label does. The catch: our survey never asks where someone was born. Pulling it from the Census or the Fed means merging datasets whose geographic boundaries and survey weights don't line up.

The fix is to ask directly. Onboarding already builds a profile, so it can collect country of origin and years in the U.S. first-hand. Until that pool is large enough, Census figures guide what we build, but they don't back claims about any specific group.

MVP Scope

Gamification, credentialing, social features can wait. The first release only has to prove that a lesson tuned to someone's income, state, and life stage lands differently than generic advice.

The MVP is scoped down to just the personalization loop:

  • Secure user profiles
  • Literacy and behavior assessment
  • Personalized learning path
  • Local context by region
