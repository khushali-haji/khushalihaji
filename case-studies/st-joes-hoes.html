The Premise

Four strangers, one address.

In a time not far from now, before current immigration policy shifts, before billions were spent to build mass detention camps, and millions of immigrants faced uncertain futures…

A French-Gabonese dancer, a painter from Mumbai, a photographer from Delhi, and a Korean-American writer walked into a former nunnery in Times Square.

It's not the start of a joke — it’s the start of the documentary. It’s set in a crumbling Catholic nunnery-turned-women’s dormitory with a rat-prone kitchen, a stained-glass chapel, and communal showers with clogged drains. Each woman came here for one reason: to make it in New York City, all before their two years of affordable housing are up.

Artistic Approach

The Fifth Character

The film is quiet and intimate, using handheld camera footage. Character arcs are interwoven, while the nunnery becomes a fifth character — creaking floors, wooden crosses, old statues.

The tone is observational and playful.

Director's Note

Living the Story

I didn’t just make this film. I lived it. St. Joseph’s was my first home in the city. I met these women in our shared kitchens and late-night conversations, and they became my first community in New York.

With a background in international reporting, urban design, and scriptwriting, I’ve always been drawn to layered stories where politics and intimacy overlap. This story is deeply personal, because in many ways, I’m one of these women.

The Themes

Making it in 2025

It's undoubtedly an old story told many times over, artists making it in New York City. But what does “making it” mean in 2025? We’re living in a tense climate where immigrant is a politically-heavy word.

These women’s stories become a testament to a time before current policy shifts, when immigrants could come with greater security into a country where they felt like they had a shot at their dreams.

This isn’t a film about trauma, stereotyping or identity politics. It’s a film about tenacity, changing and growing, in proximity, in discomfort, in community.
UX & Ethics

Accountability Maintained

The film was shot in a communal hostel where I also lived. This shared space naturally fostered trust, and the film’s participants were individuals with whom I already had personal relationships. From inception through to the final cut, they were consistently informed and engaged.

Prior to every interview, I communicated the topics we would discuss and emphasized that they could skip questions or stop at any time. If discomfort arose, the camera was turned off immediately.

I maintained regular check-ins to make sure each participant felt comfortable. At no point was anything filmed, edited, or included without full and ongoing consent.
